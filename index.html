Gethai
Fuse

A patent-pending product for Hai — a smart-shower brand in the US — that releases beauty products directly into the shower's water stream. A precision mechanism inside a deliberately simple form.

Role

  • Mechanical design lead
  • Client and manufacturer relationship management
  • DFM, validation, production trials
  • Patent application support

Collaboration

  • Beauty product manufacturers
  • Tooling partners (Asia)
  • Hai marketing team
  • Packaging and unboxing design

Constraints

  • Water-tight seal under pressure
  • Free rotation while installed
  • Valves engage on bulb attachment
  • Cosmetic finish across two material families

The Fuse exists at an unusual intersection — water pressure engineering, cosmetics chemistry, and a consumer unboxing experience that has to feel inevitable. Beauty products meet plumbing rarely, and the Fuse needed to do so reliably, in a form that read as premium the moment it left the box.

I led the mechanical design and managed two parallel relationship tracks: Hai's marketing and brand teams on one side, the beauty product manufacturers and Asian tooling partners on the other. Two engineering ideas shaped the product. The first is rotational — once the Fuse is locked onto the shower arm, the outer stainless body can be freely rotated around a fixed internal core. The cutaway shows the locked element through the centre; the housing spins around it, sealed and operational at any orientation. The second is the valve actuation: the internal valves were designed to engage as the bulb is placed onto the body, and disengage as it's removed. Loading a cartridge is the same gesture as opening the flow.

The product launched into the Hai range. Patent pending.

Caroma
Contura

A shower handset for the Caroma Contura Collection — a redesign in everything but the spray-pattern concept. The variable-spray engine that produces multiple flow patterns from one nozzle array carried over from earlier Caroma work; the nozzle geometry, internal architecture, and cosmetic form were all new, tuned and re-tested to AS standards. Recognised in the Good Design Awards.

Good Design Awards · Contura Collection

Role

  • Mechanical design and delivery — handset
  • Support across the Contura Collection
  • Standards modelling and validation

Technical

  • SolidWorks (from Rhino skin import)
  • VJet variable-spray architecture
  • AU/NZ water-contact compliance
  • Standards test modelled in CAD

Results

  • First-round standards test pass
  • Good Design Awards recognition

Caroma's variable-spray engine — a single-nozzle-array architecture that shifts spray patterns from one geometry rather than swapping nozzles — provided the technical foundation. Everything else was new. Each nozzle position and direction was individually designed against the spray-pattern targets, the internal architecture was reworked end-to-end, and the cosmetic form was built from a Rhino skin supplied by the design team.

To de-risk a short development window I modelled the AS standards test directly in SolidWorks before any physical prototype existed. That gave the team confidence in the geometry and let us commit to tooling earlier than usual. The handset passed first-round standards testing, and the Contura Collection went on to be recognised in the Good Design Awards.

Dave
PE Safety Beam

Full redesign of the PE Safety Beam sub-system for Automatic Technology's Dave — the flagship commercial door operator and winner of the Australian Good Design Award. Two legacy SKUs consolidated into one unit, with new PCB architecture, IP66 sealing, maximised battery life, and a dramatically simpler installation experience.

Australian Good Design Award · Dave system

Role

  • End-to-end Product Lead — PE Safety Beam
  • Mechanical architecture and DFM
  • Electronics co-design (with elec team) — new PCB
  • Battery / power architecture
  • Regulatory and validation

Outcomes

  • Full PE Beam redesign — new PCB, new housing
  • Two legacy SKUs absorbed into one unit
  • Shared housing across TX (transmitter) and RX (receiver)
  • Footprint reduced 25 %
  • IP66 ingress protection
  • Maximised battery life within shipping constraints
  • Significantly simpler install on-site

Compliance

  • Strict regulatory and safety standards
  • Local and international freight regulations
  • Transit testing on PE Beam components

Dave is Automatic Technology's new flagship commercial door operator, recognised with an Australian Good Design Award. I led the PE Safety Beam sub-system — the photo-electric pair that watches the threshold and stops the door on obstruction. Safety-critical, regulated, and the part of the system every installer touches. The brief was full redesign: consolidating two legacy products in completely different industrial designs — a flat wireless module and a taller wired unit — into one SKU.

New PCB co-designed with the electronics team, IP66 sealing, and a shared exterior housing between TX (transmitter) and RX (receiver) — differing only in the optics behind a common lens window. One mould, one fascia, one cosmetic spec. Footprint down 25 % out of rigorous DFM.

The most satisfying improvement was on the installation side. The beam drops into a concave seat on a sliding bracket; one wingnut secures the beam, locks its rotational alignment, and clamps the slider laterally — three jobs, one fastener, no separate adjustment screws. The new optics carry a much wider alignment tolerance than the legacy parts, so the installer doesn't need to be precise to get a reliable signal. Wireless-first, one-button pairing, and battery service every two years is one screw.

Battery was a constrained optimisation — higher-capacity chemistries pulled toward longer service life, international freight regulations on lithium pulled back the other way. Alkaline let us ship standard freight globally while delivering the service life the product needed.

Forward — Dave 2.0

Dave launched as a roller-door operator. The platform was capable of running tilt doors too — an entirely separate door category — except that a single sub-assembly stood in the way. I was asked to solve it. I redesigned and prototyped the sub-assembly, proving out tilt-door operation on the existing platform. One focused change opening a second market for the same product, effectively doubling addressable scope. Prototype-stage work.

F&P
Integrated Column Refrigerator

Sub-system lead on the installation experience and associated parts for F&P's flagship integrated refrigeration product — a built-in appliance that has to vanish into a high-end kitchen perfectly, then survive a decade of cyclic door loads.

Role

  • Sub-system lead — installation parts & process
  • Mechanical design — door, hinge structures
  • Validation testing programmes
  • Cosmetic acceptance criteria

Focus Areas

  • Installer ergonomics on-site
  • Cabinet-to-appliance trim interface
  • Premium fit and finish at the joinery
  • Hinge cyclic durability under load

Context

  • F&P flagship refrigeration
  • High-volume manufacturing in Thailand
  • Long-haul international freight (premium spare parts validation)

Integrated Column Refrigerators are the top of F&P's refrigeration line — built to disappear into a high-end kitchen behind matched timber or stone joinery, then run reliably for fifteen years while the family slams the door several hundred times a week. The brief looks simple from the showroom floor and is anything but at the bracket level.

My focus was the installation experience — the parts and the process by which a real tradie, on a real kitchen floor, gets the product mounted, levelled, aligned to the cabinetry, and looking factory-perfect. Done well, the appliance vanishes into the kitchen. Done poorly, every flaw is visible at a metre's distance and the customer notices forever.

The work spanned a printed bracket prototype on MDF mock-ups, in-situ rigs under real timber cabinetry checking the trim-to-bracket interface for cosmetic marks during door operation, and ergonomic checks of the install action itself — cordless drill, real screws, real kneeling installer. Every change to the bracket or fixing had to be tested against both the engineering case and the human case.

I also owned hinge structure work and the packaging validation for international spare parts shipments, defining cosmetic acceptance criteria so premium door and panel components arrived undamaged after long-haul freight. Validation extended to weighted-door cyclic testing in the F&P lab, simulating loaded-bin door operation across the product's expected service life.

The work doesn't photograph as cleanly as a shower handset, but it's where appliance engineering actually happens — in the unseen interfaces, the install procedures, and the testing that makes a fifteen-year warranty defensible.

F&P
Quad-Door Re-brand

Taking a proven Haier refrigerator platform and making it a Fisher & Paykel — redesigned doors and handles, a new brushed finish, and the cross-factory cosmetic engineering to make the badge credible.

Role

  • Lead — front-facing parts
  • Door and handle design
  • Finish selection and specification
  • On-site build trials, China

The Work

  • F&P design language on a fixed platform
  • New brushed cosmetic finish
  • Cosmetic matching across two factories
  • DFM within existing architecture

Manufacturing

  • Haier factory — China
  • F&P factory — Thailand
  • Mixed-origin parts on one product face

Platform sharing is how the appliance industry works — but making a Haier read as a Fisher & Paykel is harder than it sounds. The parts a customer actually touches — the doors, the handles — carry the entire brand. Everything behind them was staying; everything in front had to change.

I led the front-facing redesign: new doors and handles in F&P’s design language, and a new brushed finish specified for the range. The finish was the crux. Brushed surfaces are unforgiving — grain direction, gloss level and tone all drift between suppliers — and this product’s face assembled parts made in two factories in two countries: Haier’s lines in China and F&P’s in Thailand. A mismatch a supplier calls acceptable is one a customer reads as cheap.

Closing that gap meant working it at the source — on-site build trials in China, checking parts against masters on the line, and locking the cosmetic criteria into specification so the match held at volume, not just on the golden sample.
